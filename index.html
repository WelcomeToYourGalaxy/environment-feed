What this is. A feed of environmental destruction worldwide, filtered for scale. Nothing here is written or summarised — headlines and snippets are the publishers' own, every row links to the original, and no model touches the text.

How scale is measured. Being about the environment is not enough. Each story is scored on five signals: global scope (worth 2), regional scope such as a basin, continent or ocean (1), a systemic mechanism — tipping point, collapse, cascade, irreversible threshold, mass extinction, a global assessment (2), a magnitude such as a percentage, an area, a tonnage or a population figure (1), a human consequence such as deaths, displacement, food or water security (1), and a generalising finding — a study, assessment or dataset rather than an incident (1). Below two points a story never enters the feed. At four or more it is marked as full planetary scale. The pips on each row show the score and the words beside them name the signals it scored on.

What that excludes. A council approving recycling bins, a beach cleanup, a newly described beetle, a single-site population survey: all scored and all dropped. What survives is the finding at scale — freshwater species down 85% since 1970 and the food supply that depends on them, air pollution and its eight million deaths a year, coral bleaching across 84% of the world's reefs.

Region. Each story is placed by the ground it concerns, not the wire it arrived on: a Japanese outlet reporting on the Amazon files under Latin America. A finding with global scope, and one story can carry several regions — a study spanning Africa and South Asia appears under both. A worldwide finding that names no particular country, and anything else naming no place at all, sits under No single region rather than being assigned one it does not have. The Wire row beside it filters by where the story was read from, which is a different question: analysis desks, field press, journals, institutions, regional press, planet-scale searches.

Window. The wire keeps 45 days. The last 24 hours, 7 days and 30 days each have their own view, and Older than 30 days shows only what has aged past a month — it appears once there is anything in it.

Translation. The Translate control at the top renders the headlines and snippets on screen into any of 26 languages, through a free public translation endpoint — no account, no key, nothing stored. A translated row is marked as machine translation and keeps the publisher's original wording on hover, because a machine rendering is not the publisher's words. If the endpoint is unavailable the row simply stays in its original language rather than emptying. Translation applies to what is on screen; scroll further or widen a filter and press the control again.

Region. Placement runs three levels deep. Pick a region and a second row opens with its subregions; pick one of those and a third row lists the places inside it — Latin America to the Amazon Basin to the Brazilian Amazon, Oceania to Australia to the Northern Territory. Each row counts what it holds, so an empty subregion is visible rather than hidden, and every story files under the subregion and region above whatever place it names.

Limits worth knowing. Scoring is mechanical: it reads words, not meaning, so an important local story written without any scale language will be dropped, and a thin story dressed in scale language can slip through. Google News caps a query at roughly 100 results over about 30 days. A regional edition often carries a syndicated wire story rather than local reporting, which inflates a language count without adding a local view. Coverage is uneven by language and the counts show it rather than hiding it.

Every wire read on the last harvest: what it returned, and how many of its stories were dropped for being too local. A wire that could not be reached says so rather than disappearing.
